Research

My research focuses on labor economics, the economics of crime, and public economics. I use administrative data and quasi-experimental methods to study how criminal justice institutions and public policies affect individual behavior and economic outcomes.

Job Market Paper

Police Discretion and Pre-Arrest Diversion: Evidence from Florida

Solo authored Draft coming soon
Abstract
Misdemeanor offenses account for a large share of criminal justice contact, and even low-level encounters can have lasting consequences for defendants. This paper examines the causal effect of initial noncustodial police processing, rather than custodial arrest, on diversion access, case outcomes, and subsequent criminal justice contact. Using administrative data from Florida's Eighth Judicial Circuit, I study an adult pre-arrest diversion program administered by the State Attorney's Office, where police officers determine the initial processing route but do not directly decide diversion participation. To address the endogeneity of officers' decisions, I use an instrumental-variables design exploiting variation in officers' propensity to process eligible misdemeanor encounters noncustodially. The results show that initial noncustodial processing increases the probability of a pre-arrest diversion intake meeting by 40.3 percentage points and diversion entry by 33.3 percentage points. Although the estimates suggest a reduction in subsequent arrests within two years, the effects on subsequent criminal justice contact and index-case outcomes are not precisely estimated. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of police discretion at the earliest stage of the criminal justice process and demonstrate how initial police decisions can influence access to prosecutor-administered diversion programs.

Regulating Screening Technology: Distributional Effects of Pre-Employment Polygraph Test Bans

with Yiyu Xing (Draft available upon request)
Abstract
This paper studies the labor-market consequences of restricting employers’ use of pre-employment polygraph tests. The project is motivated by a broader research question: when policymakers delete a screening signal from the hiring process for protecting worker rights, do they reduce discrimination and improve allocation, or do they instead push employers toward noisier substitutes that can worsen outcomes for some groups? States adopted different restrictions before the federal Employee Polygraph Protection Act of 1988, and the federal statute itself created clear occupational exemptions. The analysis combines linked monthly CPS data from 1976–1988 with CPS ASEC data from 1977–1988. The linked monthly CPS allows me to study worker-level transitions into employment and into involuntary job loss, including recent-hire separations as a measure of post-hire match quality. The CPS ASEC extends the analysis to employment status, job search, income, poverty, and insurance coverage. Preliminary results suggest that polygraph bans did not mechanically improve worker outcomes.
